In a cave in northern Spain, there is a pile of bones that should not make sense.

The site is called Sima de los Huesos. The Pit of Bones. It sits inside the Atapuerca cave system, and the people in it died around 430,000 years ago. Not modern humans. An older branch of the family, pre-Neanderthal, living through a cold stretch of the Ice Age in Europe.

Here is what is strange. When researchers went through those bones, they found damage that arrives in cycles. Growth that stops and then starts again. Seasonal marks. Signs of a body that was not getting what it needed, over and over, in the same rhythm, year after year.

It looks like the pattern you see in animals that hibernate.

So a study put forward the idea that these people, when the food ran out and the winter got long, might have gone into caves and slowed themselves down. Not full hibernation like a bear. Something closer to a starving sleep, and then they waited for spring.

Other scientists pushed back hard on that. There are plenty of reasons bone growth can stall, and none of them have to involve hibernation. So I am not going to pretend this is settled. It is not.

Here is what is settled. Everything else in this video.

So, the plan. Six jobs. Six jobs the human body cannot do in an Ice Age winter. Stay warm. Cut and build. Wear something. Sleep somewhere. Eat. And stay together, through months of dark, without going insane or getting eaten.

Every one of those jobs needed a fix, and the fixes are some of the best things our species has ever made. By the end, you will know why a hole in a bone matters more than a mammoth, and you will know what those bones in Spain are actually telling us.

Job one. Stay warm.

Humans have been using fire for an extremely long time. The working estimate is at least 300,000 to 400,000 years of it. By the middle of the last Ice Age, fire was not a bonus. It was the difference between a night and a last night.

And they were far more deliberate about it than you would guess. Archaeologists digging Ice Age fire pits in Ukraine turned up rough little campfires, exactly as expected. Then they found built fireplaces. Constructed on purpose, laid out on purpose, and one of them had burned at over 600 degrees Celsius. That is hotter than 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit. That is not a campfire. That is a kiln.

When wood ran short out on the open steppe, they burned bone and animal fat instead. Fire cooked their food, hardened wooden tools, and bought them hours of light in a season where the sun barely shows up at all.

Warmth solved, as long as you never leave. Which is a problem, because you have to leave.

Job two is the one everybody skips, and it is the one every other job depends on. Tools.

Somewhere between 22,000 and 18,000 years ago, in what is now France, Spain and Portugal, people were making flint work that is genuinely hard to believe. We call it Solutrean, and the signature piece is a point shaped like a leaf.

In 1874, near the village of Volgu in France, workers uncovered a cache of these. The best one is around 35 centimeters long. Over a foot of flint, thinned almost flat, with the edges worked so finely that the first archaeologists to see it did not believe it was ancient. Getting that shape out of rock requires pressure flaking, where you push off tiny chips with a pointed tool instead of striking, and it is unforgiving. One bad chip and you start over.

You need a point like that to bring down something large. You need a blade to cut hide. You need a scraper to soften that hide so it stops being a board and starts being clothing. Every single item in the rest of this video begins as a rock that a person shaped on purpose.

Job three. Wear something.

The first version was the obvious one. Take the hide off an animal that was already built for the cold, and put it on yourself.

You can see it in the bones. In northern Europe, there are bear paw bones from at least 300,000 years ago carrying cut marks in exactly the places you would slice to pull a pelt off clean. Somebody was skinning bears for the fur, not for the meat.

But a loose hide draped over your shoulders leaks heat out of every gap. And an Ice Age winter finds every gap. The fix for that is tiny, and it is the most underrated object in this entire story.

The eyed needle. A thin sliver of bone or ivory, with a hole drilled through the end.

The earliest ones we have come from Denisova Cave in Siberia, around 40,000 years ago, and a 2024 paper in Science Advances traced eyed needles spreading across northern Eurasia right around then. A needle means seams. Seams mean fitted sleeves, cuffs that close at the wrist, layers you can move in. There is a real difference between draping an animal over yourself and wearing it. One of those keeps you alive on a long hunt.

And then there is the detail that makes people wince. Before heading out, hunters would smear animal fat, tallow, across their face and neck. It seals the skin, shuts down wind chill, and slows heat loss right where you lose it fastest. Gross. Effective.

So take stock. They had fire hot enough to be a kiln. They had cutting tools good enough to be art. They were wearing fitted animal skin with fat smeared on their faces.

That covers you for maybe three days of an Ice Age winter. Not three months.

Because cold was never the real killer. The real killer is that everything I just described only works if you have somewhere to come back to. And an Ice Age hands you a landscape with no caves and almost no trees.

Which brings us to the invention that is the closest thing to a miracle on this list. In central Ukraine, people built houses out of the skeletons of animals that outweighed them ten to one. The bones in one of those structures date to between about 18,250 and 17,750 years ago, which means that spot was in use for over four hundred years.

Here's the part of this story that never makes the museum display.

You've seen the photograph. A ring of mammoth bones out on the Ukrainian steppe, arranged like something ritual, and it looks crude. It looks like the last resort of people with no caves and no trees.

Then you find out what the doorway was doing, and "crude" stops being the right word.

The site is Mezhyrich, in central Ukraine. Four dwellings. A 2025 radiocarbon re-dating study concluded the main structure was in use for as long as 429 years. The same spot. Four centuries. Nobody rebuilds a bad idea for four hundred years.

The frame was mammoth jawbones, stacked and interlocked, with tusks arched over the top like ribs. Hides went over that, fat side out. And notice the order there — structure on the inside, insulation on the outside. That's not an accident. That's a decision somebody made and then kept making.

But the entry is the real evidence. These weren't bubbles with a flap over the hole. You went in through a long, low passage. Crawl in, and the wind is already off you. The cold air you dragged in behind you sinks and drains along the floor instead of rolling over the hearth where people are sleeping. Cold air is heavy. They knew that in their hands long before anyone could write it down.

So that's the answer to the guess I asked you for. The doorway wasn't a door. It was a filter.

And then they did the thing that makes me laugh every time. If your house is a dome of bone and hide, snow is not your enemy anymore. Snow is free. You pile it up the outside and you've just added a metre of the best insulator in the entire landscape. Arctic builders were still doing exactly this a few thousand years ago, because the physics never changed.

So they're warm, they're dry, they've got a fire burning like a kiln. Which raises the question that ends every comfortable moment in the Ice Age. What exactly are you eating in February?

Job five. Eat.

Getting food in an Ice Age winter is not a hunting problem. It's an arithmetic problem. One reindeer is three days of calories for one family, and you need a hundred and twenty days of winter.

So they stopped hunting one animal at a time. At the Rock of Solutré, in eastern France, the deposits sit against a limestone cliff and record use by four Paleolithic cultures across roughly twenty-five thousand years — horse bone on top of horse bone, in layers. The site was found in 1866 by Adrien Arcelin and Henry Testot-Ferry, and it's still one of the densest concentrations of horse remains in Europe. The working explanation is a drive: push a herd against ground it can't climb, and the landscape does the killing.

Then the harder half of the job. Not getting meat. Keeping it.

Freezing, drying, smoking, burying meat in cold ground and sinking it into cold water. And every one of those methods is really the same idea: hold the food at a temperature where bacteria give up. They didn't know bacteria existed. They knew which hole in the ground ruined meat and which one didn't. That's three hundred thousand years of trial and error doing the work of a thermometer.

Job six is the one that isn't a technology at all, and it might be the most important.

Body heat. Not a metaphor — literally the cheapest fuel source available, and it scales beautifully. One person in a hide is warm. Six people in a hide are a furnace, and the smaller the space, the fewer calories you burn staying alive.

Which means group living wasn't a cultural preference. It was a survival calculation. Get isolated in an Ice Age winter and you're not just lonely. You're burning your own body at the most expensive rate on the menu.

And you can read the priority in who gets fed. When food runs short, the people a group protects first tell you exactly what that group thinks it needs to keep going. That logic is cold. But it kept the line alive.

Because here's the thing I'm not going to skip, and plenty of Ice Age documentaries do.

At Gough's Cave in Somerset, England, there are human remains from about 14,700 years ago. A 2015 study found human tooth marks on the bones. Not animal marks — human ones. Three skulls had been carefully worked into cups.

I'm not going to tell you a tidy story about that. Specialists still argue about whether it was ritual, or hunger, or something that was both at once. What I can tell you is what it means for this episode. They spent hundreds of thousands of years building the greatest survival toolkit our species has ever assembled, and a bad enough winter could still beat it. This isn't a story about conquering the Ice Age. It's a story about staying one step ahead of it.

Okay. Breathe. Because the next part is genuinely ridiculous.

You want to know how long humans used living animals as central heating? Thousands of years after the Ice Age, European farmers were still building houses with the cows on the ground floor and the family upstairs. In parts of Scandinavia and the Alps, this was standard architecture for centuries. The body heat from the herd came up through the floorboards. The house was the animal.

Imagine explaining that to a heating engineer. "So the boiler is downstairs." "Great, gas or oil?" "Holstein."

And the honest part is that it worked. Warm floor, free fuel, and a smell you simply stopped noticing. Which tells you something about comfort — it's whatever your neighbours also have.

One more piece of equipment, and I want to be straight about the dates here, because this one is often the wrong way round.

Snow goggles. A curved plate with a narrow slit, worn so your eyes survive a landscape made of reflected sunlight. Inuit and Yupik makers cut them from whalebone, driftwood, walrus ivory, antler — whatever floated or died nearby. The Canadian Museum of History describes examples recovered from an early Inuit house on Baffin Island.

Here's the honest note. This evidence is much later than the Ice Age, and it's Arctic rather than European. But the problem it solves is ancient: glare off snow can literally blind you, and a blinded hunter on a glacier in February is a dead hunter. The slit version is the fix, and once you've seen it you cannot unsee it.

And now the plot twist.

Everything up to this point has been a story about inventing our way out of the cold. Tools, houses, needles, goggles. People solving a problem with an object.

Then you meet the Yaghan.

The Yaghan lived in Tierra del Fuego, at the bottom of South America, in freezing wind and near-freezing water. Darwin saw them in the 1830s during the Beagle voyage and was genuinely baffled — people living through that climate with almost nothing on. A 2021 study using bone density and genomic analysis described the Fuegians as an exemplary cold-adapted population. Reviews of circumpolar populations have found elevated resting metabolic rates as a recurring pattern across cold-climate groups.

They didn't beat the cold with a cupboard full of gear. Their bodies ran hotter. The furnace got turned up at the factory, over generations, in the same species you're in.

Which reframes everything before it. The inventions weren't the only strategy. They were the strategy for people whose bodies couldn't change fast enough. The Yaghan are what the other path looks like.

So. Back to Spain. Back to the bones that started this.

In 2020, a study in the journal *L'Anthropologie* proposed that the Sima de los Huesos hominins, around 430,000 years ago, may have survived hard winters in a state like hibernation — a slowed, starving sleep, waiting out the worst of it.

And I told you at the start that I wouldn't pretend it was settled, so here's the honest version. It isn't. Other researchers point out that interrupted bone growth has several mundane explanations — malnutrition, disease, ordinary seasonal stress — and that no human population is known to hibernate. The site itself is a deep shaft where bodies accumulated over time, which complicates what any of those skeletons can tell us about how the people lived.

So the answer is: maybe. Probably not in the way the headline suggests. But possibly in a way we haven't ruled out. That's where real science leaves you, and I'd rather leave you there than hand you a fact that isn't one.

Here's what isn't in doubt.

The body you're sitting in cannot do an Ice Age winter. No fur, no fat layer worth the name, no hibernation, no claws. Everything that kept our ancestors alive through hundreds of thousands of years of cold was something they worked out and then handed down: a shaped rock, a hole drilled through a splinter of bone, a house with a corridor, a cached haunch of meat, six people breathing in a small dark room.

Not one of those is impressive on its own. Together they're the reason there's a person reading this.

So next time you're cold — actually cold, waiting for a bus, and annoyed about it — notice something. You're not scared. That's new. For most of human history, cold was a thing that could kill you by Thursday, and the fact that it's now an inconvenience is a stack of unglamorous inventions and one hole in a piece of bone.

You are what those people built. Which means the least you can do with a winter is use it well.
